Enzyme inhibition comes up in almost every A Level Biology paper, often as a 3 to 5 mark "explain" question. The content isn't hard, but it's one of the easiest topics to lose marks on, because examiners want a precise chain of ideas, and most students skip a link in the chain.

This post covers what you need to know, what examiners are looking for, and what separates a 2-mark answer from a full-mark one.

## First, a quick recap: how enzymes work

An enzyme has an **active site** with a specific shape, determined by the enzyme's **tertiary structure**. Only a substrate with a **complementary** shape can bind to it, forming an **enzyme–substrate complex**. The enzyme lowers the activation energy of the reaction, the products are released, and the enzyme is free to be used again.

An **inhibitor** is any molecule that reduces the rate of an enzyme-catalysed reaction. Every inhibition answer comes back to one idea: **fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form per unit time**. Keep that in mind, because it's the link students most often leave out.

## Competitive inhibition

A competitive inhibitor has a **similar shape to the substrate**. That means it can fit into the **active site** and bind there, which blocks the substrate from entering.

The inhibitor and substrate are **competing** for the same active sites. Which one wins depends on how many of each molecule are around:

- At **low substrate concentration**, inhibitor molecules occupy many active sites, so the rate falls a lot.
- At **high substrate concentration**, substrate molecules vastly outnumber the inhibitor and are much more likely to collide with and occupy active sites. The effect of the inhibitor is overcome, and the reaction can still reach the **same maximum rate (Vmax)**.

Because more substrate is needed to reach any given rate, the **Michaelis–Menten constant (Km) increases**. Km is the substrate concentration at which the rate is half of Vmax, so a higher Km means the enzyme appears to have a lower affinity for its substrate.

**Example:** malonate is a competitive inhibitor of succinate dehydrogenase, an enzyme in the Krebs cycle. Malonate is similar in shape to the enzyme's substrate, succinate.

## Non-competitive inhibition

A non-competitive inhibitor **does not bind to the active site**. It binds somewhere else on the enzyme, often called an **allosteric site**.

Binding there **changes the tertiary structure** of the enzyme, which **changes the shape of the active site**. The substrate is no longer complementary to the active site, so it can't bind, and fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form.

Because the inhibitor isn't competing with the substrate for the active site, **adding more substrate doesn't overcome the inhibition**. The enzyme molecules affected by the inhibitor are effectively out of action, so **Vmax is lower**. Km is usually shown as **unchanged**, because the enzyme molecules that are still working behave normally.

**Example:** cyanide inhibits cytochrome c oxidase, the final enzyme in the electron transport chain, which is why it stops aerobic respiration.

## Side by side

| | Competitive | Non-competitive |
|---|---|---|
| Where it binds | Active site | Elsewhere on the enzyme (allosteric site) |
| Shape of inhibitor | Similar to the substrate | Not similar to the substrate |
| Effect on active site | Blocked, but shape unchanged | Shape changed |
| Increasing substrate concentration | Overcomes the inhibition | Does not overcome the inhibition |
| Vmax | Unchanged | Decreased |
| Km | Increased | Unchanged |

## Reading the graph

You'll often be given a graph of rate of reaction against substrate concentration, with three curves: no inhibitor, competitive inhibitor, and non-competitive inhibitor.

- **No inhibitor:** rate rises steeply, then levels off at Vmax.
- **Competitive inhibitor:** rate rises more slowly, but **eventually reaches the same plateau** as the uninhibited curve at high substrate concentrations.
- **Non-competitive inhibitor:** rate rises and levels off at a **lower plateau**, which never reaches the uninhibited Vmax, however much substrate is added.

When asked to identify the type of inhibitor from a graph, always quote the evidence: "The curves reach the same maximum rate at high substrate concentration, so the inhibitor is competitive."

## Reversible, irreversible and end-product inhibition

Most inhibitors you meet at A Level are **reversible**: they bind temporarily with weak bonds and can leave the enzyme. Some inhibitors are **irreversible**: they bind permanently, often by forming covalent bonds, so the enzyme is permanently inactivated.

**End-product inhibition** is where the final product of a metabolic pathway inhibits an enzyme earlier in the pathway, often non-competitively. When the product builds up, it slows its own production. When it's used up, the inhibition is removed and production speeds up again. This is a form of **negative feedback**, and it stops cells wasting energy and raw materials making something they already have enough of.

## The exam question

Here's the kind of question you'll see:

> **Explain how a non-competitive inhibitor reduces the rate of an enzyme-catalysed reaction. [4]**

### What examiners credit

The mark scheme will usually contain more points than there are marks, and you get one mark for each point you make clearly, up to the maximum. For this question, creditworthy points are:

1. Inhibitor binds to the enzyme at a site **other than the active site** (allosteric site)
2. This changes the **tertiary structure** of the enzyme
3. The **active site changes shape**
4. The substrate is **no longer complementary** / can no longer bind
5. **Fewer enzyme–substrate complexes** form
6. Increasing substrate concentration **does not overcome** the inhibition

### A weak answer (1 or 2 marks)

> The inhibitor binds to the enzyme and changes its shape so the substrate can't fit into it anymore. This means the reaction slows down.

What's wrong with it:

- "Binds to the enzyme" doesn't say **where**. Examiners want "not at the active site."
- "Changes its shape" is too vague. The mark is for the **active site** changing shape, and ideally the **tertiary structure** being altered.
- "Can't fit into it" loosely gets at the substrate not binding, but "no longer complementary" is the precise idea.
- There's no mention of **enzyme–substrate complexes**, so the link between shape and rate is missing.

### A strong answer (4 marks)

> The non-competitive inhibitor binds to the enzyme at a site other than the active site, called an allosteric site. This alters the tertiary structure of the enzyme, so the shape of the active site changes. The substrate is no longer complementary to the active site and cannot bind, so fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form, and the rate of reaction decreases. Increasing the substrate concentration does not overcome this inhibition.

This answer makes five creditworthy points in four sentences, so even if one isn't accepted, it still gets full marks. That's the aim: make more points than there are marks.

## Mistakes that cost the most marks

**Saying the enzyme is "denatured."** Denaturation is caused by high temperature or extreme pH breaking bonds in the tertiary structure. A reversible inhibitor changes the active site's shape, but examiners won't accept "denatured" for inhibition.

**Saying the inhibitor "kills" the enzyme.** Enzymes are proteins, not living things. They can't be killed.

**Saying the substrate and active site have the "same shape."** They are **complementary**, not the same. Similarly, a competitive inhibitor has a shape **similar** to the substrate, which is why it's complementary to the active site.

**Forgetting the enzyme–substrate complex.** "The substrate can't bind, so the rate decreases" skips a step. Say that **fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form**.

**Mixing up which inhibitor is overcome by more substrate.** Only competitive inhibition is overcome by increasing substrate concentration, because the substrate out-competes the inhibitor for the active site.

## Quick check

Try answering these before looking back:

1. Why does a competitive inhibitor need a shape similar to the substrate?
2. On a graph, how can you tell a non-competitive inhibitor from a competitive one?
3. What happens to Km with a competitive inhibitor, and why?
4. Why is end-product inhibition useful to a cell?
