Cells are constantly talking to each other. Some of that talking happens through hormones and nerve signals, but cells also send something much stranger: tiny packages called **exosomes**.

## What are exosomes?

Exosomes are extremely small bubbles, or **vesicles**, released by cells. They're only about 30 to 150 nanometres across, around a thousand times thinner than a human hair, so they can only be seen with an electron microscope.

Each exosome is surrounded by a membrane, just like a cell, and carries cargo from the cell that made it: **proteins**, **lipids**, and different kinds of **RNA**, including small molecules called microRNAs that can switch genes on or off.

## How are they made?

Exosomes form inside the cell. Part of the cell's membrane folds inwards to make a compartment, and inside that compartment, even smaller bubbles form, trapping bits of the cell's contents. When the compartment fuses with the cell's outer membrane, those tiny bubbles are released outside the cell. Those are exosomes.

## What do they do?

When exosomes were first studied in the 1980s, scientists thought they were just a way for cells to get rid of waste. We now know they're also a way for cells to **send messages**. When another cell takes in an exosome, the cargo can change how that cell behaves.

This is useful in the body. For example, immune cells use exosomes to share information about infections. But it can also cause harm: cancer cells release exosomes that can help prepare other parts of the body for the cancer to spread.

## Why scientists are excited

Exosomes are found in blood, saliva and urine, and they carry clues about the cells that made them. That means a simple blood test might one day detect diseases like cancer early, by reading the messages in exosomes. This idea is sometimes called a **liquid biopsy**.

Researchers are also exploring whether exosomes could be used to **deliver medicines** to specific cells, since cells naturally take them in.

One word of caution: this research is still early. Some clinics sell "exosome treatments" for things like ageing or joint pain, but most of these aren't approved or proven, and regulators like the US FDA have warned people about them.

## Quick recap

- Exosomes are tiny membrane-bound packages released by cells.
- They carry proteins, lipids and RNA from one cell to another.
- They help cells communicate, in both healthy and harmful ways.
- They could lead to new blood tests and new ways of delivering drugs.
