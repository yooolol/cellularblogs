Almost everything happening inside you right now depends on enzymes. Digesting your last meal, copying your DNA, releasing energy from glucose: each step is carried out by an enzyme, a protein that speeds up a chemical reaction without being used up.

Enzymes are incredibly fast. Some can process thousands of molecules every second. But that raises an important question: how do you slow one down or switch it off? The answer is **enzyme inhibition**, and it turns out to be behind everything from painkillers to poisons.

## How enzymes work

Each enzyme has a small pocket called the **active site**. Its shape is very precise, and only molecules with a matching, **complementary** shape can fit into it. These molecules are called the **substrate**.

When the substrate fits into the active site, they form an **enzyme–substrate complex**, the reaction happens, and the products are released. The enzyme is then free to do the same thing again, over and over.

An **inhibitor** is a molecule that interferes with this process, so fewer reactions happen. There are two main ways it can do this.

## Competitive inhibition: taking someone's seat

A competitive inhibitor has a shape **similar to the substrate**, so it can slot into the active site. While it's sitting there, the real substrate can't get in.

Think of it like musical chairs: the inhibitor and the substrate are competing for the same seats. If you add lots more substrate, it wins most of the seats, and the enzyme can work at almost full speed again.

**A real example:** statins, some of the most widely prescribed medicines in the world, are competitive inhibitors. They block an enzyme called HMG-CoA reductase, which the liver uses to make cholesterol, so less cholesterol is produced.

**Another one:** if someone swallows methanol (found in some antifreeze), an enzyme in the liver converts it into toxic products that can cause blindness. One old emergency treatment is giving ethanol, because ethanol competes with methanol for the same enzyme's active site, slowing down the production of those toxic products while the body gets rid of the methanol.

## Non-competitive inhibition: changing the lock

A non-competitive inhibitor doesn't go into the active site at all. It binds somewhere else on the enzyme, often called an **allosteric site**.

When it binds, it changes the enzyme's overall shape, and that changes the shape of the active site. The substrate no longer fits, so the reaction can't happen. Because the inhibitor isn't competing for the active site, adding more substrate doesn't help.

**A real example:** cyanide blocks an enzyme called cytochrome c oxidase, which cells need for the final stage of aerobic respiration. With that enzyme blocked, cells can't use oxygen to release energy, which is why cyanide is so deadly so quickly.

## Permanent vs temporary inhibition

Most inhibitors are **reversible**: they bind loosely and can let go again. Some are **irreversible**: they bind permanently, so the enzyme molecule is out of action for good, and the body has to make new ones.

**Aspirin** is an irreversible inhibitor. It permanently blocks COX enzymes, which make chemicals involved in pain, inflammation and blood clotting. That's why a small daily dose of aspirin is sometimes prescribed to reduce the risk of blood clots.

**Penicillin** works the same way against bacteria. It permanently blocks the enzymes bacteria use to build their cell walls. Without strong walls, the bacteria burst. Our own cells don't have cell walls or those enzymes, which is why penicillin can kill bacteria without harming us.

## Cells use inhibition too

Inhibition isn't just something done *to* cells. Cells use it to control themselves.

Many substances are made through a chain of reactions, each step handled by a different enzyme. Often, the **final product** of the chain acts as an inhibitor of an enzyme near the **start** of the chain. This is called **end-product inhibition**.

When there's plenty of the product, it slows its own production. When the product gets used up, the inhibition wears off, and production speeds up again. It's a built-in thermostat, a type of **negative feedback**, that stops the cell wasting energy making things it already has enough of.

A good example is respiration: when a cell has plenty of ATP (its energy currency), ATP itself inhibits an enzyme called phosphofructokinase early in the process, slowing respiration down until more energy is needed.

## Why this matters

Once you understand enzyme inhibition, you start seeing it everywhere:

- **Medicines** such as statins, aspirin, antibiotics and many antiviral drugs work by inhibiting specific enzymes.
- **Poisons** such as cyanide are deadly because they shut down enzymes we can't live without.
- **Our own cells** use inhibition to keep thousands of reactions balanced every second.

Designing a new drug often starts with a simple question: which enzyme do we need to block, and what shape of molecule would fit?

## Quick recap

- Enzymes speed up reactions; the substrate fits into the **active site**.
- **Competitive inhibitors** look like the substrate and block the active site. More substrate can overcome them.
- **Non-competitive inhibitors** bind elsewhere and change the active site's shape. More substrate can't overcome them.
- Some inhibitors are **reversible**, others **irreversible**.
- Cells use **end-product inhibition** to control their own chemistry.
